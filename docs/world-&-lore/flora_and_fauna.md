# Flora and Fauna

> **Purpose:** A field reference for the living environment of *ULTIMA AETAS*. Specific species, ranges, and seasonal behavior should be adapted to the region used by the game; this guide establishes ecological and gameplay principles rather than a finalized species checklist.

## A world still alive

The collapse damaged habitats and interrupted human care, but it did not stop ecological change. Plants colonize neglected lots, animals alter their routes around noise and danger, and weather affects what can be gathered or traveled through. Nature is not automatically safe, pristine, or hostile. It is a changing system on which survivors depend without fully controlling it.

## Flora

### Useful plants

Wild greens, berries, nuts, roots, and medicinal plants may provide food or practical materials when correctly identified and gathered in season. Availability depends on location, weather, soil, competition, and prior harvesting. A familiar plant is not necessarily safe in every condition, and knowledge can vary between characters.

Foraging should reward observation and preparation. The game should distinguish a known, verified resource from a guess, and avoid treating all plants as universally edible or medicinal.

### Regrowth and succession

- Unmowed verges, empty gardens, and disturbed ground can be overtaken by grasses, weeds, and young growth.
- Vines, moss, roots, and moisture can weaken neglected structures over time.
- Abandoned orchards and gardens may remain productive, but their condition depends on age, weather, access to water, and whether anyone still tends them.
- Seasonal cycles affect visibility, food availability, fire risk, and the condition of travel routes.

### Ecological hazards

Thorns, irritating sap, toxic berries, poisonous fungi, allergens, and contaminated water can make a seemingly useful place dangerous. Hazards should have discoverable cues where practical, and severe outcomes should follow clear rules rather than arbitrary surprise.

## Fauna

Wildlife reacts to its needs and surroundings. Animals may seek water, food, cover, mates, or safer ground; their behavior can change with season, human activity, and nearby infected. Their presence can reveal that a habitat still supports life, but it does not guarantee that the area is safe.

### Roles in the world

- **Prey animals** can provide food and materials, but hunting costs time, noise, equipment, and effort. Their populations should not behave as an unlimited resource.
- **Predators and scavengers** may avoid people, investigate remains, or defend territory. They are part of the ecology, not interchangeable combat encounters.
- **Birds and small animals** can provide subtle environmental signals through calls, sudden silence, tracks, nests, or movement. Such signs are clues, not infallible alarms.
- **Domestic animals** may be feral, sheltered, lost, or still cared for. Their condition reflects the people and places around them.

### Infected and wildlife

The infected are a distinct survival threat, not wildlife. Their presence may change animal movement and access to habitat, but the exact effects should follow established game rules. Do not imply that every animal is infected or that ordinary ecological behavior can diagnose infection with certainty.

## Survival and ecology systems

Flora and fauna can support grounded survival gameplay when they connect to the wider simulation:

- Time of year and recent weather influence growth, water, animal activity, and travel.
- Gathering and hunting consume time and can create noise or leave visible traces.
- Repeated harvesting affects what remains available nearby.
- A resource's value depends on knowledge, tools, storage, preparation, and the needs of the community.
- Encounters can be avoided, observed, or misunderstood; not every animal interaction needs to become combat.
- Ecological change should be legible through signs in the environment and remembered by people who rely on local knowledge.

## Field-note template

Use this template when adding a species or plant to the setting:

- **Common name:** What locals call it; note regional variations.
- **Habitat and range:** Where it appears and what conditions it needs.
- **Seasonality:** When it is visible, useful, or difficult to find.
- **Signs:** Tracks, calls, scent, damage, growth patterns, or other observable clues.
- **Human use:** Food, materials, medicine, livestock, or no known use.
- **Risks and uncertainty:** Misidentification, contamination, defensive behavior, or disputed knowledge.
- **World effects:** How its presence or absence affects travel, resources, and local routines.

## Design safeguards

Favor plausible, regionally coherent ecology over a universal catalog. Treat local knowledge as valuable but not infallible. Avoid making every plant a resource and every animal a threat. The natural world should create opportunities, limits, and atmosphere—and continue changing whether or not the player is watching.
