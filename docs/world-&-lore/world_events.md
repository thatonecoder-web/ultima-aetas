# World Events

> **Purpose:** A framework for events that make the world feel as if it continues beyond the player's immediate view. Dates and named incidents are intentionally left open until the campaign timeline is finalized.

## Event principles

World events are consequences of conditions, not random spectacle. They should emerge from weather, resources, travel, community decisions, the infected, and the passage of time. An event can begin off-screen, but its effects should be discoverable through changed routines, damaged places, missing supplies, fresh tracks, new notices, or conflicting testimony.

The player may influence an event without controlling every outcome. Some events resolve before the player learns of them. Others remain unresolved until people make new choices.

## Event categories

### Environmental change

- Storms, floods, heat, cold snaps, and seasonal shifts alter routes and shelter needs.
- A damaged levee, washed-out crossing, fallen tree, or frozen pump can isolate a community.
- Changes in vegetation and animal movement can signal changing conditions without guaranteeing what comes next.
- Environmental effects persist: a flooded store may spoil food; a cleared road may become easier to travel and easier to watch.

### Community pressure

- A shortage prompts rationing, trade, migration, or disagreement over who receives help.
- A repair succeeds or fails depending on parts, skills, time, and cooperation.
- A leadership change can alter rules and relationships without instantly changing everyone's loyalties.
- New arrivals bring needs, knowledge, and uncertainty. Their presence can strengthen a settlement and strain it at once.

### Human conflict and cooperation

- A dispute over a route, resource, promise, or past harm can grow if ignored or cool through mediation.
- A shared threat may bring rival groups together temporarily without erasing their history.
- A trade agreement, rescue, or act of betrayal can travel as rumor, changing how people receive the player elsewhere.
- Communities may choose to relocate, close their gates, or send people out to seek help.

### Infected activity

- Noise, light, weather, obstacles, and prior movement can draw infected toward places people rely on.
- A disturbance can displace infected rather than eliminate the danger; routes and risks change over time.
- Infected encounters should remain grounded in observable behavior and established game rules. Avoid introducing a sudden explanation or new ability solely to force a plot beat.

### Personal turning points

- A character leaves, returns, changes their routine, or asks for help.
- A rumor is corrected—or becomes more entrenched despite evidence.
- A private loss becomes a public event, or a public decision creates private consequences.
- A promise comes due after the player has moved on.

## Event lifecycle

Use this lightweight structure when designing a persistent event:

1. **Pressure:** Establish the underlying condition, such as a low supply, worsening weather, or unresolved grievance.
2. **Trigger:** Identify what makes the condition visible or urgent.
3. **Response:** Give affected people distinct options based on their needs, relationships, and resources.
4. **Consequence:** Record changes to people, places, resources, routes, and beliefs.
5. **Echo:** Let the result surface later through memory, rumor, altered routines, or follow-on events.

Not every event needs all five stages to happen on screen. The world should retain enough evidence for the player to understand that something occurred, while allowing uncertainty about details.

## Event record template

When tracking a significant event, record:

- **Event name:** A concise identifier used by the design team.
- **When and where:** Relative time, location, and affected routes.
- **Known facts:** What can be established in the world.
- **Conflicting accounts:** Who believes what, and why accounts differ.
- **People affected:** Immediate participants and those affected later.
- **State changes:** Resources, access, safety, relationships, and community decisions.
- **Player involvement:** What the player could know or influence, including if they were absent.
- **Continuity hooks:** Evidence and consequences that can appear in later play.

## Continuity safeguards

- Keep the exact cause and timeline of the collapse consistent with the dedicated timeline once established.
- Do not imply that every crisis is caused by the player or exists to deliver a quest.
- Preserve room for incomplete information; rumors should not silently become objective facts.
- Let recovery and deterioration coexist. A community can repair one part of life while losing another.
- Make meaningful changes persist long enough to be remembered, and avoid resetting consequences without an in-world reason.
